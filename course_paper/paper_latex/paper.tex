\section{Introduction}

Routability is a critical aspect of ASIC synthesis and layout. Placement optimization, whether analytical or SA-based, requires a cost function to evaluate candidate solutions, which ideally reflects the true routability. Since actual routing is computationally expensive and time consuming when it comes to large-scale designs especially in ASIC design, more accurate and efficient routability estimation methods are always in demand. Traditional estimator for this problem often fall into the "fast or accurate" dilemma. Fast estimators, such as HPWL estimation, correlate with routability but often ignore the capacity constraints and design rules, leading to inaccurate results. On the other hand, detailed estimators, such as global routing based methods, such as PathFinder \cite{mcmurchie1995pathfinder}, are accurate but often very expensive for iterative placement. Therefore, there is a need for routability estimation methods that are both global-router accurate and HPWL efficient. 

Recent advances in machine learning (ML) have shown promising results in various domains, including EDA development. ML-based approaches are able to learn complex mapping from placement features to detailed routing outcomes, which shows its diversity. And its "once trained, inference fast" nature is suitable for repeated evaluation such as inner loop of simulated annealing.

To explore and analyze the ML approaches for this problem, This paper reviews three methodologies from 2014 to 2022 that show the progression of ML in this field: from expert-driven feature engineering, to architectures borrowed from computer vision, to domain-specific designs that respect circuit structure.

\section{Problem Definition}

In ASIC physical synthesis, routing proceeds in two stages. Global routing first tessellates the chip into a grid of rectangular gcells, where each gcell represents a coarse region with limited routing capacity, which is the number of metal tracks available for wires to pass through. 
The global router then constructs a routing resource graph over this grid and finds coarse paths for all nets, typically using algorithms like PathFinder that iteratively negotiate congestion by increasing costs on overused gcells. Once global routing completes, detailed routing takes over: it realizes actual metal geometries within each gcell, connecting pins with wires and vias while satisfying design 
rules such as minimum spacing, minimum width, via enclosure, and metal density constraints. The challenge is that congestion estimated during global routing often fails to predict detailed routing outcomes, because global routing does not model pin access blockages, via stacking effects, or the full complexity of design rules.

The congestion prediction problem aims to estimate detailed routing outcomes earlier in the flow. All three approaches reviewed in this paper share this goal, but differ in their exact formulations. The MARS(Multivariate Adaptive Regression Splines \cite{friedman1991mars})-based approach \cite{qi2014accurate} takes placement plus global routing results (segment counts, via counts, pin statistics) as input, and output per-gcell utilization and DRC violation counts as regression targets. RouteNet \cite{xie2018routenet} takes placement alone, encoded as a multi-channel image (RUDY (Rectangular Uniform wire DensitY)-based wire density \cite{spindler2007rudy}, pin density, macro regions), and outputs either an overall routability classification or per-gcell hotspot predictions. LHNN \cite{wang2022lhnn} also works from placement, but represents the circuit as a lattice hypergraph preserving netlist connectivity, outputting both demand regression and binary congestion classification. These differences reflect the field's evolution: 1. earlier work required global routing results, while later methods predict directly from placement. 2. output formulations shifted from regression toward classification as the community recognized that identifying congestion hotspots is more actionable than predicting exact utilization values. Due to these differing formulations and benchmarks, it is impossibile to compare three approaches directly and numerically, which is why this paper focuses on comparing their methodologies rather than ranking their performance.

\section{MARS-based Approach}

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.7\columnwidth]{MARS.png}
\caption{MARS-based congestion prediction framework.}
\label{fig:mars}
\end{figure}

The first approach \cite{qi2014accurate}, proposed by Qi et al. in 2014, follows a traditional
machine learning philosophy: domain experts design features based on
their understanding of what causes routing congestion, then a regression
model learns the mapping from these features to congestion outcomes.
The key part is not actually the model itself but the feature engineering, which is the careful selection
of features that capture factors the traditional congestion models miss or cannot fully take into account.

\subsection{Feature Engineering}

Traditional congestion models use a simple mapping:
\begin{equation}
f: N \rightarrow R
\end{equation}
where the number of wire segments $N$ maps to resource utilization $R$.
This ignores many factors. The authors propose a richer mapping:
\begin{equation}
f: (N_{seg} \times N_{via} \times \Phi_{pin}) \rightarrow (N_{DRC}, R)
\end{equation}
where segment count, via count, and pin distribution data $\Phi_{pin}$
together predict both DRC violation count and utilization.

The authors identify two main sources of detailed routing congestion
that global routing typically underestimates. The first is local pin
access. Every pin needs a short wire to connect it to the routing
tracks, and these access paths consume routing resources on lower
metal layers. When pins are densely packed or have small shapes,
accessing them becomes harder and causes more congestion. The second
source is via-related blockage. Vias connect wires across metal layers,
and their enclosure rules block nearby routing tracks. Stacked vias
behave differently from non-stacked ones.

Based on this understanding, the authors extract features for each
gcell. For pin-related information: pin count $n_{pin}$, pin shape
size $A_{pin}$, and pin neighboring metric $\rho_{neighbor}$. The
neighboring metric measures local crowding:
\begin{equation}
\rho_{neighbor} = \frac{A_{blocked}}{A_{window}}
\end{equation}
where $A_{window}$ is a region extending one routing pitch around the
pin, and $A_{blocked}$ is the area within that window occupied by
other pins or blockages. For routing-related information from global
routing, they count: side-connecting segments, through-segments,
stacked vias $n_{stack}$, and non-stacked vias $n_{nonstack}$. Since
a gcell may contain multiple pins, they compute statistical
aggregations—mean $\mu$, standard deviation $\sigma$, and top-$k$\%
averages rather than just totals.

Different metal layers have different design rules, so the authors
train separate models for each layer. A model for Metal2 will have
different coefficients than one for Metal5.

\subsection{Learning Algorithm}

For the learning algorithm, the authors choose Multivariate Adaptive
Regression Splines (MARS). MARS builds models of the form:
\begin{equation}
\hat{f}(x) = c_0 + \sum_{i=1}^{N} c_i \prod_{j=1}^{d_i} h_{ij}(x_{ij})
\end{equation}
where each $h_{ij}(x)$ is a hinge function: either $\max(0, x - t)$
or $\max(0, t - x)$ for some threshold $t$. The product terms with
degree $d_i > 1$ capture variable interactions. For example, high
pin count combined with high via count may cause more congestion
than either factor alone. Unlike neural networks, MARS requires no
hyperparameter tuning, and the output is a readable expression.

Once trained, the model integrates into global routing. During path
search, the router queries the model to get predicted congestion for
each gcell. In PathFinder terms, the node cost $C_n$ becomes:
\begin{equation}
C_n = \begin{cases}
utilization & \text{if } n_{DRC} = 0 \\
1 + \frac{n_{DRC}}{N_m} & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $N_m$ is a normalization constant. This replaces the traditional
overflow-based cost with a learned prediction of detailed routing outcome.

\subsection{Results and Limitations}

The approach was tested on DAC 2012 benchmarks at 45nm. When integrated
into a global router, it reduced DRC violations by 34-37\% and via count by up to 13\%, depending on the routing flow configuration. Feature importance analysis showed pin count and segment
counts were most influential, confirming the authors' intuition.

However, this approach has limitations. It requires global routing
results as input, which means it cannot predict from placement alone. Also, the features
are designed for a specific technology and may need re-engineering
for different processes. Moreover, while MARS captures non-linearity, it
may miss patterns that experts did not anticipate.

\begin{algorithm}[h]
\caption{MARS-based Congestion Prediction}
\label{alg:mars}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Placement $P$, Global routing result $GR$
\Ensure Per-gcell DRC prediction, utilization prediction

\State \textbf{Feature Extraction:}
\For{each gcell $g$}
    \State Extract pin features: $n_{pin}$, $A_{pin}$, $\rho_{neighbor}$
    \State Extract routing features from $GR$: segment counts, via counts
    \State Compute statistics: $\mu$, $\sigma$, top-$k$\% averages
\EndFor

\State \textbf{Model Training (per metal layer):}
\State Collect labeled data: $(features, DRC_{actual}, util_{actual})$
\State Train MARS model: $\hat{f}(x) = c_0 + \sum_i c_i \prod_j h_{ij}(x)$

\State \textbf{Integration into Global Routing:}
\For{each PathFinder iteration}
    \For{each gcell $g$ in path search}
        \State $n_{DRC}, util \gets \hat{f}(features_g)$
        \State $C_g \gets util$ if $n_{DRC}=0$, else $1 + n_{DRC}/N_m$
    \EndFor
    \State Update paths using predicted costs
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{RouteNet: Image-based Approach}

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.7\columnwidth]{RouteNet.png}
\caption{RouteNet image-based architecture.}
\label{fig:routenet}
\end{figure}

The second approach \cite{xie2018routenet}, RouteNet proposed by Xie et al. in 2018, takes a
fundamentally different direction: instead of hand-crafting features,
treat the circuit layout as an image and let a convolutional neural
network (CNN) learn the patterns automatically. This borrows proven
architectures from computer vision, where CNNs have dominated image
recognition tasks since 2012.

\subsection{Image Representation}

The core insight of this approach is that placement data naturally forms a 2D grid
structure—similar to pixels in an image. The authors represent the
layout as a multi-channel image. For overall DRV (Design Rule Violations) prediction, three
channels are used:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Macro regions}: binary mask indicating macro blockages
    \item \textbf{Long-range RUDY}: wire density from nets spanning
          longer than a threshold
    \item \textbf{RUDY pins}: pin density weighted by connected nets' RUDY
\end{itemize}

RUDY extends HPWL by spreading
each net's wire demand uniformly within its bounding box. For the
$k$-th net with bounding box corners $(x^k_{min}, y^k_{min})$ to
$(x^k_{max}, y^k_{max})$:
\begin{equation}
w^k = x^k_{max} - x^k_{min}, \quad h^k = y^k_{max} - y^k_{min}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
RUDY^k(x,y) \propto \begin{cases}
\frac{w^k + h^k}{w^k \times h^k} & \text{if } (x,y) \in \text{bounding box} \\
0 & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
The total RUDY at any location sums over all nets:
\begin{equation}
RUDY(x,y) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} RUDY^k(x,y)
\end{equation}
Long-range RUDY includes only nets above a distance threshold, as
these correlate more strongly with DRV.

\subsection{Network Architecture}

RouteNet addresses two prediction tasks with different architectures
and different inputs. For overall DRV prediction, which works from
placement alone without routing, the authors use ResNet-18 pretrained
on ImageNet. The input tensor $X_i \in \mathbb{R}^{w \times h \times 3}$
is resized to $224 \times 224 \times 3$ to match ImageNet dimensions.
Placements are grouped into four classes $c_0, c_1, c_2, c_3$ based on
DRV quartiles, and training uses only extreme classes ($c_0$ and $c_3$)
for cleaner decision boundaries.

For DRC hotspot detection, RouteNet uses global routing congestion
as an additional input feature. The output must be a dense map
indicating hotspot locations—not a single score. This requires a
fully convolutional network (FCN):
\begin{equation}
f_{hotspot}: \mathbb{R}^{w \times h \times F} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^{w \times h}
\end{equation}
The FCN uses two pooling layers to downsample feature maps to
$\frac{h}{4} \times \frac{w}{4}$, then transposed convolutions to
upsample back to full resolution. A shortcut connects the 2nd layer
directly to the 7th layer, preserving both local detail and global
context. The loss function combines pixel-wise error with L2
regularization:
\begin{equation}
Loss = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \sum_{m,n} ||f_{hotspot}(X_{imn}) - Y^{clip}_{imn}||^2 + \lambda||W||^2
\end{equation}
where $Y^{clip}$ clips extreme DRV values to reduce outlier effects.

The core design of this approach is about the receptive field. A
congestion hotspot in one gcell may be caused by distant macros or
routing demand—the detailed router may detour around congestion
elsewhere. Traditional methods like SVM or logistic regression extract
features from local windows; experiments found $5 \times 5$ gcells
optimal, but accuracy remained limited. CNNs expand receptive field
through pooling: deeper layers aggregate information from larger
regions. Further ablation studies confirm this: removing pooling layers
significantly degrades accuracy.

\begin{algorithm}[h]
\caption{RouteNet \#DRV Prediction}
\label{alg:routenet}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Placements $\{X_i \in \mathbb{R}^{w \times h \times 3}\}_{i=1}^{N}$, Targets $\{y_i\}_{i=1}^{N}$
\Ensure Trained model $f_{\#DRV}$

\State \textbf{Preprocess:}
\For{each $i \in [1, N]$}
    \State Resize $X_i$ to $X_i^{\#DRV} \in \mathbb{R}^{224 \times 224 \times 3}$
\EndFor
\State Find quartiles $q_1, q_2, q_3$ from $\{y_i\}$
\State Assign class $C_i \in \{c_0, c_1, c_2, c_3\}$ by quartile
\State Training set $\gets \{(X_i, C_i) \mid C_i = c_0 \text{ or } C_i = c_3\}$

\State \textbf{Training:}
\State Load pretrained ResNet-18
\State Replace output layer for single score
\State Train with MSE loss, SGD optimizer, $\sim$30 epochs
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Results and Limitations}

RouteNet was tested on ISPD 2015 benchmarks with mixed-size designs.
For overall DRV prediction (without routing), it achieves accuracy
similar to global routing while being orders of magnitude faster—under
one second per placement. For hotspot detection (with GR features),
it improves true positive rate (TPR) by 50\% over global routing at matched false positive rate (FPR). Both
tasks outperform SVM and logistic regression baselines.

However, the image representation has a fundamental limitation: it
discards netlist topology. RUDY and density maps encode spatial
distribution, but not which cells share the same net. A net spanning
distant gcells creates correlated congestion at both ends, but an
image-based model sees only spatial proximity, not topological
connectivity, which is addressed directly by the next approach.

\section{LHNN: Graph-based Approach}

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{LHNN.png}
\caption{LHNN lattice hypergraph neural network architecture.}
\label{fig:lhnn}
\end{figure}

The third approach \cite{wang2022lhnn}, LHNN proposed by Wang et al. in 2022, addresses 
the fundamental limitation of image-based methods,which is neglecting
netlist topology. The key insight is circuits are not images, 
they are hypergraphs. A net connecting multiple pins creates dependencies 
across all the gcells it spans. When we convert a circuit to RUDY maps 
or density images, this connectivity information is lost. Two gcells 
may both show high wire density, but the image does not tell us whether 
they share the same net and will experience correlated congestion.

Consider a concrete example: a net spans gcells at opposite corners of 
the chip. In an image representation, these gcells are distant pixels. 
For a CNN to relate them, information must propagate through many 
convolutional layers—and even then, the network does not know they 
belong to the same net. In contrast, LHNN represents this net as a 
hyperedge connecting both gcells directly. Information flows between 
them in a single message-passing step, regardless of physical distance.

\subsection{LH-Graph Formulation}

LHNN introduces the Lattice Hypergraph (LH-graph), which combines two 
graph structures. The lattice graph captures spatial adjacency: gcells 
that are physical neighbors can share information. The hypergraph 
captures topological connectivity: gcells covered by the same net can 
share information. Together, they preserve both the 2D layout structure 
and the netlist structure.

Formally, the LH-graph is defined as $G = (V_c, V_n, A, H)$ where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $V_c \in \mathbb{R}^{N_c \times d_c}$: features for $N_c$ gcells
    \item $V_n \in \mathbb{R}^{N_n \times d_n}$: features for $N_n$ nets
    \item $A \in \mathbb{R}^{N_c \times N_c}$: lattice adjacency matrix, 
          where $A_{ij} = 1$ if gcells $i$ and $j$ are spatial neighbors
    \item $H \in \mathbb{R}^{N_c \times N_n}$: hypergraph incidence matrix, 
          where $H_{ij} = 1$ if gcell $i$ is covered by net $j$
\end{itemize}

This formulation parallels the hypergraph representation used in 
partitioning algorithms like hMetis \cite{karypis1999hmetis}, where nets are hyperedges 
connecting multiple cells. The difference is that LHNN uses this 
structure for learning, not optimization.

Each net node carries four features: vertical span $span_V$, horizontal 
span $span_H$, pin count $npin$, and bounding box area $Area = span_H 
\times span_V$. Each gcell node carries net density, pin density, and 
a terminal cell mask. Importantly, conventional features like RUDY can 
be recovered from this graph by simple aggregation:
\begin{equation}
RUDY_i \approx \sum_{j: H_{ij}=1} \frac{npin_j \times (span_H^j + span_V^j)}{Area_j}
\end{equation}
This shows that the graph formulation subsumes hand-crafted features 
while enabling the network to learn richer representations.

\begin{algorithm}[h]
\caption{LHNN Congestion Prediction}
\label{alg:lhnn}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Placement $P$, Netlist $N$
\Ensure Per-gcell congestion classification, demand regression

\State \textbf{Graph Construction:}
\State Build lattice adjacency $A$: $A_{ij}=1$ if gcells $i,j$ adjacent
\State Build hypergraph incidence $H$: $H_{ij}=1$ if gcell $i$ in net $j$
\State Initialize $V_c^{(0)}$ (gcell features), $V_n^{(0)}$ (net features)

\State \textbf{Encoding Phase:}
\State $V_c^{(1)} \gets \phi_c(f_c(V_c^{(0)}) \| H \cdot f_n(V_n^{(0)}))$ \Comment{FeatureGen}

\For{$l = 1$ to $L_{hyper}$} \Comment{HyperMP blocks}
    \State $V_n^{(l)} \gets$ aggregate from gcells via $H^\top$ (topological)
    \State $V_c^{(l)} \gets$ aggregate from nets via $H$ (topological)
\EndFor

\For{$l = 1$ to $L_{lattice}$} \Comment{LatticeMP blocks}
    \State $V_c^{(l)} \gets$ aggregate from neighbors via $A$ (geometric)
\EndFor

\State \textbf{Joint Learning Phase:}
\State $\hat{y}^{reg} \gets MLP_{reg}(V_c^{(L)})$ \Comment{demand regression}
\State $\hat{y}^{cls} \gets MLP_{cls}(V_c^{(L)})$ \Comment{congestion classification}
\State $\mathcal{L} \gets MSE(\hat{y}^{reg}, y^{reg}) + BCE_\gamma(\hat{y}^{cls}, y^{cls})$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Network Architecture}

The LHNN architecture has two phases: encoding and joint learning. 
The encoding phase uses three types of message-passing blocks, each 
serving a distinct purpose.

The \textbf{FeatureGen block} initializes the learning process. It 
transforms raw features through MLPs and performs an initial aggregation 
from nets to gcells:
\begin{equation}
V_c^{(1)} = \phi_c^{(1)}\left(f_c^{(1)}(V_c^{(0)}) \| H \cdot f_n^{(1)}(V_n^{(0)})\right)
\end{equation}
where $\|$ denotes concatenation. This gives each gcell an initial 
representation that includes information from all nets covering it.

The \textbf{HyperMP blocks} expand the receptive field topologically. 
They alternate message passing in two directions: gcells aggregate 
information to their nets (via $H^\top$), then nets distribute 
information back to their gcells (via $H$). After one HyperMP block, 
each gcell knows about other gcells that share any of its nets. After 
two blocks, it knows about gcells two hops away in the hypergraph. 
This is the key mechanism that allows distant but topologically 
connected gcells to influence each other's predictions.

The \textbf{LatticeMP blocks} expand the receptive field geometrically. 
They pass messages between spatially adjacent gcells using the lattice 
adjacency matrix $A$. This captures local spatial patterns that the 
hypergraph might miss. For example, congestion spreading to neighboring 
gcells even if they do not share nets.

This architecture uses 2 HyperMP blocks followed by 3 LatticeMP blocks, 
with hidden dimension 32 throughout.

\subsection{Joint Learning}

The second phase is joint learning phase, which addresses a practical problem. One could 
train a model to predict routing demand (regression), then threshold 
the predictions to identify congested gcells. But this works poorly 
because demand values in congested and non-congested regions are often 
very close, meaning small prediction errors lead to many misclassifications.

Instead, LHNN jointly predicts both routing demand and congestion:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{reg} + \mathcal{L}_{cls}
\end{equation}
The regression loss is standard MSE. The classification loss uses 
weighted binary cross-entropy with a factor $\gamma \in (0,1]$ that 
reduces the contribution of non-congested gcells, addressing the class 
imbalance (roughly 83\% of gcells are non-congested). The regression 
task provides a richer training signal; the classification task produces 
the actionable output.

\subsection{Results and Insights}

LHNN was evaluated on ISPD 2011 and DAC 2012 benchmarks, with placements 
from DREAMPlace and ground truth from NCTU-GR 2.0. It achieves over 
35\% improvement in F1 score compared to image-based models like U-net 
and Pix2Pix.

The ablation studies reveal what matters most: removing HyperMP edges 
causes the largest performance drop. This confirms that topological 
message passing, not just deeper networks, is essential. It is also interesting that 
a simple 4-layer MLP on local features performs comparably to U-net, 
suggesting that CNNs' geometric receptive field expansion adds little 
value without topological structure.

The methodology actually aligns with a principle from lecture 9, which is algorithms that 
exploit problem structure outperform generic approaches. Just as hMetis 
exploits hypergraph connectivity for partitioning, LHNN exploits it for 
congestion prediction.

\section{Critical Analysis}

The three approaches represent a progression in how to model circuits 
for machine learning. The core question is not which model to use 
(MARS, CNN, or GNN), but rather what information about the circuit 
to preserve during learning. Table~\ref{tab:comparison} summarizes 
the key differences; here we focus on the trade-offs each methodology 
makes.

It is well demonstrated from the course material that good algorithms exploit problem structure. 
PathFinder succeeds by modeling routing as negotiated resource sharing. 
hMetis succeeds by exploiting hypergraph connectivity for partitioning. 
The evolution from MARS to LHNN reflects this principle: image-based 
methods impose a 2D grid structure that discards the circuit's true 
hypergraph nature. LHNN's improvement suggests that preserving netlist 
topology is essential. Moreover, ML-based congestion prediction can be 
viewed as learning a better cost function, one that approximates detailed 
routing outcomes at placement-time speed, filling the gap between 
fast-but-inaccurate HPWL and accurate-but-slow global routing.

\subsection{Trade-offs}

\textbf{Interpretability vs. Accuracy.} MARS produces a readable 
expression; one can inspect coefficients and identify which features 
matter (e.g., pin count has importance 30, segment count 29). Neural 
networks are black boxes, which means we know they work, but cannot easily explain 
why a particular gcell is predicted congested. For integration into 
existing CAD flows, interpretability aids debugging and trust.

\textbf{Prediction Timing.} The MARS-based approach requires global 
routing results, limiting its use to refining GR decisions. RouteNet 
and LHNN predict from placement alone, enabling earlier intervention. 
This "predict early" capability is valuable: catching congestion before 
routing saves significant runtime in iterative optimization.

\textbf{Generalization.} MARS features are technology-specific as features 
designed for 45nm may not transfer to 7nm. RouteNet uses ImageNet 
pretraining but the domain gap between natural images and circuits 
is large. LHNN's graph structure is arguably more universal. Netlists 
are hypergraphs regardless of technology node. However, this remains to be validated across 
diverse industrial designs and technologies as this is a realitively new reaseach.


\subsection{Limitations and Future Directions}

All three approaches share limitations: they require expensive ground 
truth from detailed routing for training. Currently, they are validated primarily on 
academic benchmarks rather than industrial designs with billions of 
cells, and predict congestion without directly optimizing it. 

Future directions from these works include richer graph structures incorporating timing 
and power, multi-task learning for specific DRC types, and tighter 
integration with placement optimization algorithms. 

Additionally, global awareness remains a challenge. LHNN addresses this through 
hypergraph connectivity, but further improvements may come from \textbf{attention mechanisms}, such as Graph Attention Networks \cite{velickovic2018gat} (GAT) or 
Transformer-style self attention, which could further improve global awareness. 
The evolution from CNNs to hypergraph message passing shows that 
capturing long-range dependencies is critical; attention mechanisms 
offer another path to achieve this by learning which nets or gcells 
are most relevant for predicting congestion at each location, rather 
than treating all neighbors equally during aggregation.


\section{Conclusion}

This paper surveyed three machine learning approaches for ASIC routing 
congestion prediction, tracing a methodological evolution from 
expert-crafted features (MARS, 2014), to image-based CNNs (RouteNet, 2018), 
to domain-specific hypergraph networks (LHNN, 2022). 

The central insight from this progression is that representation matters 
more than model complexity. LHNN's 35\% improvement over CNNs comes not 
from a more powerful architecture, but from preserving the hypergraph 
structure that defines circuit connectivity. This aligns with a recurring 
theme in CAD: algorithms succeed when they respect problem structure. This ideology has pushed the approches borrowed from other field (e.g., computer vision) to more domain-specific designs.

As for future directions, the field is moving toward earlier prediction, richer representations, 
and mechanisms for global awareness, whether through hypergraph topology 
or attention-based learning, the ultimate goal remains open: not just 
predicting routability, but using learned models to fully guide or even automate the placement and 
routing toward manufacturable designs \sout{and we will all be out of jobs then}.
